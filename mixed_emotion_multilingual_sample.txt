The hallway held its breath after midnight. Mara heard three knocks from the locked nursery, then a child's voice whispered, "Je suis là," even though the room had been empty for twelve years. She wanted to run, but the old house seemed to lean closer, listening for the sound of her fear.

At breakfast, Ravi tried to act brave by attacking the haunted toaster with a butter knife. The toaster shot out two slices like tiny golden warnings, and his grandmother said, "Ay, Dios mío," as if the appliance had personally offended her ancestors. Ravi bowed to it and announced that negotiations with breakfast had failed.

When Leela opened the blue envelope, the joke in her throat disappeared. Inside was her father's old train ticket, folded around a note that said, "बेटी, घर लौट आओ," and for a moment the whole noisy station softened around her. She laughed once because she did not know how else to stop herself from crying.

The ghost returned that evening, but it did not scream. It sat beside Ravi on the stairs, pointed at the burnt toast, and murmured, "C'est dommage," with such solemn disappointment that Ravi apologized to both the spirit world and the bread. Mara, still trembling, found herself smiling through tears.

By dawn, the house no longer felt hungry. Leela placed the ticket on the kitchen table, Ravi made toast under strict supernatural supervision, and Mara whispered, "Ya no tengo miedo," though her hands were still shaking. The three of them watched the sun enter the hallway like a quiet promise.
